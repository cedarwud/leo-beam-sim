# External Best Practices, Prior Art & Pitfalls for a Dual-Audience LEO Beam-Allocation 3D Simulator

## TL;DR
- **Your existing design is strongly validated by prior art**: the "toggle basic↔smart mode," per-user red/green coverage field, protagonist-plus-crowd, slowed-down handover, progressive-disclosure levels, and "you be the algorithm" mini-game each map cleanly onto documented, evidence-backed patterns from Nicky Case, Bret Victor, PhET, Distill, and the Zachtronics catalogue. The single most important refinement is to make the *aggregate* improvement (fairness/coverage) legible through **every individual dot moving at once**, not through a scalar like Jain's index.
- **The binding technical risk is your target hardware**: software-rendered WebGL (SwiftShader/llvmpipe, no GPU) is **fill-rate / fragment-bound and CPU-bound**, so the usual "InstancedMesh to cut draw calls" advice is secondary. The biggest wins are clamping devicePixelRatio to 1, disabling antialiasing, on-demand rendering, flat/unlit materials, minimizing overdraw, and pushing all text/UI into DOM/Canvas 2D overlays. Critically, Chrome has *deprecated and is removing* automatic SwiftShader fallback (noted in DevTools since Chrome 130; WebGL context creation will fail instead), and Chromium itself advises falling back to Canvas2D — so a genuine non-WebGL 2D path is mandatory, not optional.
- **For the defense**, de-risk with seeded determinism, a pre-recorded backup video, a click-through fallback, and a rehearsed "pivot line," and turn the committee's skepticism into a feature by manipulating live sliders (objective weights, capacity, user count) to rebut challenges on the spot — the "prove it live" affordance is your strongest rhetorical asset if and only if it is deterministic.

## Key Findings

1. **Explorable-explanation design is a mature craft with published, reusable patterns.** Nicky Case's "start with a hook," "let the reader form their own model," "gating/withholding to motivate," and "Do & Show & Tell" patterns; Bret Victor's "ladder of abstraction" and "control time" (scrubbing); and PhET's research-based "implicit scaffolding" and "must function intuitively or attention goes to the UI, not the concept" are directly applicable and evidence-backed.
2. **Telecom handover already has strong visual prior art**, but almost all of it is expert-facing (3GPP signaling diagrams, Ericsson L1/L2 mobility). The gap your simulator fills — a layperson-legible, story-driven handover — is real and under-served. The best borrowable metaphor is the "signal deteriorates at cell edge → switch to neighbor" arc.
3. **RL decisions can be made legible to non-experts via counterfactuals**, and there is peer-reviewed evidence that counterfactual state explanations help non-experts identify a flawed agent better than no explanation. Google's What-If Tool is the canonical deployed exemplar of "drag a slider, watch the decision flip."
4. **To make a statistical improvement visceral, show every unit.** Unit/dot charts (one dot per user) plus animated transitions between conditions are the documented technique; fairness scalars (Jain's index, Gini) should be *secondary* readouts, not the primary "aha."
5. **Productive failure is a real, replicated learning-science result**: two randomized-controlled studies (Kapur, *Cognitive Science* 2014) found that letting learners attempt a problem *before* instruction yields significantly greater conceptual understanding and transfer to novel problems. This is the evidence base for your "you be the algorithm" mini-game — let players try (and pile everyone onto one beam) before revealing the smart policy.
6. **Live-demo failure is a well-documented genre with a standard mitigation playbook**: rehearse on the real hardware, freeze the system after rehearsal, keep a pre-recorded screencast and a click-through backup, seed all randomness, and prepare a calm pivot line.
7. **On software WebGL, fill-rate dominates.** Cap DPR at 1, disable AA, render on demand, use unlit materials, minimize overdraw, and overlay 2D. Detect the software renderer at runtime and switch to a low-fidelity/2D mode — and note Chrome is removing the software fallback entirely.
8. **LEO beam-footprint visualization has excellent community prior art** (starlink.sx dynamically plots 3-dB spot-beam contours and even simulates gateway capacity; satellitemap.space and issinfo.net render the whole constellation in a single batched draw call). Energy efficiency is conventionally shown with battery/power-bar metaphors.

## Details

### Area 1 — Explorable explanations / interactive educational simulators

**Strongest exemplars and what to extract:**

- **Nicky Case, "Explorable Explanations" and "How I Make Explorable Explanations" (blog.ncase.me).** Primary source, from the person who popularized the practice. Concrete extractable patterns:
  - *Start with a hook* that requires no prior knowledge (Mathigon's Chocolate Box game). For you: open on the protagonist user losing signal — a felt problem — before any jargon.
  - *Let the explorer create their own data points and form their own model* (Angry Physics: users fire projectiles and discover the sine pattern themselves). For you: let the player assign users to beams and *discover* that greedy assignment collapses.
  - *Gating / productive withholding*: "paradoxically, by withholding an explanation, the explorable can be more effective… it gets the explorer more motivated to seek it out." Earth: A Primer blocks progress until a task is done. This validates your progressive-disclosure levels.
  - *Structure by "BUT/THEREFORE," not "and then"* (Case cites the South Park writing rule; used in The Evolution of Trust). For you: "everyone piles on one beam BUT the beam saturates THEREFORE the tail starves BUT a smart policy spreads load THEREFORE the tail is served."
  - *End with a sandbox* the reader can only appreciate after the guided portion (Parable of the Polygons, Earth: A Primer). This validates your live-sliders end state.
- **Bret Victor, "Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction" and "Explorable Explanations" (worrydream.com).** Primary source; coined the term. Key patterns: *control time* (a scrubber to slow/replay a process) — directly validates your slowed-down handover event; and *move deliberately between levels of abstraction* (one concrete user ↔ the whole field) — validates your protagonist-plus-crowd design. Victor's caution is relevant: for human-authored rule systems, "human understanding is an essential part of the design process," while for machine-learned policies "we don't expect to understand why or how they work" — so your simulator must *manufacture* the intuition an RL policy doesn't natively give.
- **PhET Interactive Simulations (University of Colorado Boulder), research papers by Adams, Perkins, Wieman et al.** Primary, peer-reviewed. The evidence base is large: Wieman, Adams & Perkins report in *Science* 322 (2008) that "we have also conducted more than 250 interviews of individual students using PhET simulations in a think-aloud format." Findings you must heed:
  - "The simulation must function intuitively or the student's attention is focused on how to use the simulation rather than on the topic presented." Your UI must be self-evident; a defense audience distracted by controls will miss the argument.
  - *Implicit scaffolding / "guiding without feeling guided"* (Paul, Podolefsky & Perkins) — design the environment so users are gently steered toward productive exploration without explicit instructions. This is the mechanism behind your progressive-disclosure levels.
  - *Help only on request*, placed near the relevant object as short sentences (Sound Waves sim). Supports your expert-label toggle being off by default.
  - Interviews showed sliders/drag controls "encourage interaction and exploration," but text-entry is preferred when a *specific* value is needed — so keep exploratory sliders but allow exact entry for a defense ("set capacity to exactly 10").
- **Distill, "Communicating with Interactive Articles" (Hohman, Conlen, Heer, Chau, 2020).** Peer-reviewed synthesis. Documents that multimedia/interactive learning shows "large effect sizes for improvement of transfer learning in many cases," but warns interactivity "may be distracting… or the functionality may go unused." Actionable: instrument and test; don't add interactivity for its own sake.
- **Bartosz Ciechanowski (ciechanow.ski).** Deployed exemplars (GPS, Internal Combustion Engine, Cameras & Lenses). Pattern: *build up from first principles*, one interactive figure per concept, conversational tone, custom-coded lightweight visuals, and a global animation pause/disable toggle (good accessibility and — critically for you — a performance escape hatch). His GPS piece even builds intuition about satellite visibility and coverage from scratch, a close domain analogue.

**Ranked shortlist (Area 1):** (1) PhET — the only body with peer-reviewed, interview-derived design rules for *simulations specifically*; (2) Nicky Case — the clearest, most directly transferable pattern language for playful teaching; (3) Bret Victor — the conceptual backbone (time control, ladder of abstraction); (4) Distill/Hohman — the academic bridge and evidence base; (5) Ciechanowski — the gold standard for progressive, first-principles build-up and an animation-pause escape hatch.

### Area 2 — Teaching networking / handover visually

- **Ericsson blog, "5G Advanced handover: L1/L2 Triggered mobility."** Industry-primary. Clear articulation of the baseline handover as monitor→measure→report→execute, and the "signal quality deteriorates at cell edge → switch to neighbor" narrative. Borrow this arc for your slowed-down handover.
- **arXiv 2504.13424 (MARL for load balancing in 5G) and 1312.6911 (QoS-aware user association).** Peer-reviewed/preprint. Confirm the domain framing you're using (user→cell association, load-balancing index) and, importantly, that **Jain's fairness index is the field-standard load-balancing metric** — so your expert-label toggle should surface it, but the layperson view should not lead with it.
- **Industry explainer videos (e.g., mohamedeladawi.com on 5G beam management/inter-cell handover).** Secondary/practitioner. Useful visual vocabulary (beam switching, SSB beams) for your expert mode.
- **Advids "Telecom & 5G Visualization" guide.** Marketing/secondary — treat its statistics ("87% positive ROI," "conversion +20-30%") as unverified vendor claims, **not** evidence. But its *design* advice is sound and echoes primary sources: "reveal information sequentially, animating one process at a time to focus the viewer's attention," and use a "Problem–Solution–Benefit" narrative. This aligns with Case's hook→build→payoff.

**Contradiction/gap to flag:** Nearly all telecom handover prior art is expert-facing (signaling ladder diagrams). There is a genuine scarcity of *layperson* handover explainers, which is both an opportunity (novelty) and a risk (no proven template to copy) — lean harder on the explorable-explanation patterns from Area 1 than on telecom-native visuals.

### Area 3 — Visualizing RL / agent-policy decisions and counterfactuals

- **Distill, "Understanding RL Vision" (2020) and "The Paths Perspective on Value Learning" (2019).** Peer-reviewed, deployed interactives. Patterns: *dissect a single failure step-by-step* ("one case of failure was caused by an obstacle being temporarily obscured from view"); and give a *visual interpretation of value* (paths perspective). For you: when the naive baseline collapses, freeze and annotate *why* a specific user starved — that single-case dissection is more convincing to a committee than an aggregate curve.
- **Distill, "The Building Blocks of Interpretability" (2018).** Peer-reviewed. Warns that impoverished interfaces (bare saliency maps) "leave a lot of value on the table" and that interfaces "should not mislead… the explicit information displayed should not cause users to implicitly draw incorrect assessments." Directly relevant to how you visualize what the agent "sees."
- **Google What-If Tool (Wexler et al., IEEE TVCG 2019; arXiv 1907.04135).** Peer-reviewed + deployed. The canonical "no coding, drag a feature, watch the prediction flip, find the nearest counterfactual" tool, explicitly built so "non-technical users" can probe fairness and counterfactuals. This is the closest deployed analogue to your objective-weight sliders and is a strong citation for "counterfactual sliders that flip a decision."
- **Counterfactual explanations for RL (Gajcin & Dusparic, ACM Computing Surveys 2024, arXiv 2210.11846; Olson, Khanna, Neal, Li & Wong, *Artificial Intelligence* 295 (2021), article 103455 / arXiv 2101.12446).** Peer-reviewed. Two load-bearing claims: counterfactual explanations are *specifically targeted at non-expert users* and give "actionable advice"; and, from Olson et al.'s Study 2 (a normal vs. a flawed agent on Atari Space Invaders), **"counterfactual state explanations have sufficient fidelity to the actual game images to enable non-experts to more effectively identify a flawed RL agent compared to the nearest neighbor baseline and to having no explanation at all."** This is your direct empirical evidence for framing the smart-vs-naive comparison as a counterfactual ("if the weight on fairness were lower, *this* user would starve").
- **Explainable RL survey (arXiv 2507.12599; earlier survey 2020).** Peer-reviewed. Caution: most XRL methods "neglect the human side," simplifying a complex model rather than designing an inherently interpretable one. Your auction/coordinated decode is an opportunity to show an *inherently* legible decision (who bid, who won the beam).

**Ranked shortlist (Area 3):** (1) Google What-If Tool — deployed, non-expert-tested, directly maps to your sliders; (2) Olson et al. counterfactual-state user study — the actual empirical evidence that counterfactuals help non-experts; (3) Distill "Understanding RL Vision" — the model for single-failure dissection; (4) Gajcin & Dusparic survey — the framing vocabulary.

### Area 4 — Making an aggregate/statistical improvement visceral

- **Unit charts / dot plots (DataViz Catalogue; "one dot per unit").** Reference/practitioner. The core technique for your coverage field: one dot per user, colored red/green, so the *aggregate* fairness improvement is read as "the field turns green," not as a number. This is exactly your existing design — prior art confirms it is the right choice.
- **Animated transitions between conditions (Gapminder / Hans Rosling lineage; NYT scrollytelling).** Deployed exemplars. Animating dots *moving* from the collapsed state to the balanced state makes the intervention's effect kinetic and memorable. FlowingData and the Information-is-Beautiful roundup document NYT scrollytelling (e.g., "342,000 Swings Later") as the gold standard of before/after data narrative.
- **Small multiples vs. animation — Brehmer, Lee, Isenberg & Choe, "A Comparative Evaluation of Animation and Small Multiples for Trend Visualization on Mobile Phones," IEEE TVCG (arXiv 1907.03919).** Peer-reviewed controlled study. Trade-off to flag precisely: small-multiples users "completed seven out of nine trend comparison tasks more quickly," while Animation users "report feeling more confident in their task responses (up to 0.76 points higher on a 1–5 scale)." Recommendation: use the animated toggle for the *emotional* "aha" and viewer confidence, and offer a static side-by-side (basic | smart) for the committee's faster *analytical* comparison.
- **Fairness scalars — Jain's index and Lorenz/Gini (multiple arXiv sources; Jain, Chiu & Hawe 1984).** Peer-reviewed/primary. Jain's index runs from 1/n (one user hogs everything — your collapse case) to 1 (perfectly equal). Show it as a *supporting* readout that ticks from ~1/n to near-1 as the field turns green; the Lorenz curve is a good expert-mode addition (it "shows the shape of the inequality," whereas the Gini/Jain scalar only summarizes it). **Do not** lead with any scalar for the lay audience — a hollow number is precisely what you're trying to avoid.

**Design pattern (explicit):** Primary "aha" = every dot turning green + dots visibly redistributing; secondary quantification = Jain's index bar + coverage %; expert-mode depth = Lorenz curve / small-multiples.

### Area 5 — Educational game mechanics

- **Productive failure (Manu Kapur).** Peer-reviewed, replicated including randomized-controlled experiments. Kapur, "Productive Failure in Learning Math," *Cognitive Science* 38(5) (2014): "Two randomized-controlled studies found that both methods lead to high levels of procedural knowledge. However, students who engaged in problem solving before being taught demonstrated significantly greater conceptual understanding and ability to transfer to novel problems than those who were taught first." Effect sizes are large — Kapur & Bielaczyc (2012) report F(2,299)=116.96, p<.001, partial η²=.44. The number of solutions a learner generates predicts learning gains. This is the rigorous justification for your "you be the algorithm" mini-game: let players attempt allocation and fail (pile onto one beam) *before* revealing the RL policy. Design implication: the task must be "intuitively accessible yet challenging," admitting multiple attempts.
- **Zachtronics catalogue + "Case Studies in Game-Based Complex Learning" (MDPI, *Multimodal Technologies and Interaction*, 2021).** Peer-reviewed case study interviewing Zach Barth. Patterns for "be the system" puzzles: *the game design is the instructional design*; *just-in-time information*; *remove busywork / whole-task practice*; *scaffold by introducing one new "wrinkle" at a time* (Barth literally bubble-sorts levels by complexity); and players "almost always play in the given order" even when free to choose — so a curated level order beats an open menu. Directly applicable to your progressive-disclosure levels and mini-game.
- **Zachtronics "Zachademics."** Primary (studio). Confirms these games are used in CS curricula to teach loops, branching, synchronization, optimization — and that "failure is safe, experimentation is fun." Human Resource Machine is the most *accessible* "be the assembly" analogue; SpaceChem/Opus Magnum for optimization-under-constraints (your beam-capacity constraint).
- **Human-vs-AI scoring.** No single strong academic source surfaced, but the Zachtronics "optimize for cycles/parts" leaderboard mechanic is the deployed template: after the player allocates, score them on coverage/fairness/energy and *then* show the RL policy's score — the gap motivates the "why is the algorithm better" question (Strogatz via Case: "help them love the questions").

**Ranked shortlist (Area 5):** (1) Kapur's productive failure — the empirical backbone for "fail first, then reveal"; (2) MDPI Zachtronics case study — evidence-based mechanics for "be the system"; (3) Zachademics — deployment proof and accessibility ladder (HRM → SpaceChem).

### Area 6 — Live interactive demos at a thesis defense

Consolidated best practices from practitioner sources (LinkedIn/Elshafei's "10 tips," Reprise's SE panels, Medium/Srinathmohan) — these are experience reports, not peer-reviewed, but strikingly consistent:

- **Rehearse on the actual hardware, in the actual room, on the actual connection.** ("Run your full demo… on the actual hardware.") For you this is doubly critical given the software-WebGL target — rehearse on a machine with the GPU disabled.
- **Freeze the system after rehearsal**: "avoid installing any updates or changing system settings until after you've delivered" (the DivX/Office anecdote where a night-before install crashed a live demo).
- **Keep a pre-recorded full screencast and a click-through backup deck.** ("Prepare a pre-recorded screencast… 'Let me just show you a quick video of how this works.'") Test the complete flow "10 minutes before, not an hour before."
- **Have a rehearsed pivot line** ("People won't judge you for using a video; they'll judge you for not having one"). Steve Jobs' 2007 clicker failure and the Windows 98 BSOD are the canonical "stay calm, have humor" cases.
- **Redundancy**: second device, backup renderer/build.

**Seeded determinism (your specific need):** Since your simulator has randomness (user positions, arrivals) and an RL policy, seed *all* randomness so the demo is reproducible run-to-run. This is both a demo-safety measure and a scientific-credibility measure — a committee can re-run the exact scenario. Determinism also lets you pre-compute the collapse so it always lands on cue.

**Turning skepticism into a feature:** Your live sliders (objective weights, capacity, user count) are the rebuttal engine. Anticipated challenge → live manipulation: "Does it only work at 100 users?" → drag user count to 150. "Are you cherry-picking weights?" → drag the fairness weight down and show the tail starve, then back up. This is the What-If-Tool interaction pattern (Area 3) deployed rhetorically. **Precondition:** it must be deterministic and fast enough on the venue hardware, or it backfires — hence the software-WebGL work in Area 7 is a defense-critical dependency, not a nicety.

### Area 7 — three.js / R3F for legible large scenes AND software-WebGL performance

**Legibility (focus+context, attention guidance):**
- Push all labels/text/HUD into **DOM overlays** — three.js CSS2DRenderer (official: "very useful if you want to combine HTML based labels with 3D objects," translation-only, screen-facing) or drei's `<Html>` (supports `occlude`, `center`, `distanceFactor`). This both guides attention (crisp typography, expert-label toggle) and offloads cost from the renderer.
- Caution (developer blog): CSS2DRenderer "updates every element every frame; at 200+ elements the DOM cost becomes significant" — with ~100 users, cap visible labels to the protagonist + nearest few, not all.
- Time-dilation/slow-motion for the handover event is a first-class explorable pattern (Bret Victor's "control time"); implement as a simple animation-clock scale, which costs nothing on the renderer.

**Software-WebGL performance (SwiftShader / llvmpipe, no GPU) — the critical constraint:**
- **Fill-rate/fragment cost dominates, not draw calls.** Software rasterizers are CPU-bound and per-pixel-bound. Diagnostic (Khronos/shadergif): "reduce resolution 50%; if FPS jumps, you're fill-bound." Emscripten's official WebGL optimization guide: "sort front to back to minimize overdraw… do a depth prepass to reduce actually-rasterized fragments to a minimum."
- **Biggest win — clamp devicePixelRatio to 1.** DPR cost is quadratic: 3x DPR = 9x the pixels of 1x. `renderer.setPixelRatio(1)` (go below 1 via a scaled canvas if still slow). (simplified.media; principle corroborated by Emscripten/Khronos.)
- **Disable antialiasing** (`antialias:false`) — removes MSAA resolve passes (pure savings on a CPU rasterizer; inferential from fill-rate principle).
- **On-demand rendering** — R3F `frameloop="demand"` (official): a near-static scene renders ~0 fps until interaction; call `invalidate()` on change. For a mostly-static coverage field this is the single largest per-frame saving; the scene only re-renders during handover animations or slider changes.
- **Unlit/flat materials** — MeshBasicMaterial over MeshStandardMaterial (PBR shading is far more per-fragment math). No shadows, no post-processing. (Inferential from fragment-cost principle + Emscripten "reduce fragment shader complexity"; not an isolated SwiftShader benchmark.)
- **InstancedMesh still helps CPU draw-call submission** for ~100 users, but is *secondary* to fill-rate; note a documented case where InstancedMesh was *slower* than plain meshes in some scenarios, so measure. Community reports show software WebGL at ~6 fps (llvmpipe aquarium demo) vs 60 fps on GPU — an order-of-magnitude penalty; treat that figure as a single WSL bug-report data point, not a controlled benchmark.
- **Detect the software renderer at runtime** via `WEBGL_debug_renderer_info` → `UNMASKED_RENDERER_WEBGL` containing "SwiftShader"/"llvmpipe"/"Software"/"SwANGLE", or use pmndrs `detect-gpu` / drei `useDetectGPU` (tier 0 = <15 fps → "provide a fallback to a non-WebGL experience"). Switch to a low-fidelity or Canvas 2D mode.
- **Strategic caveat (primary, Chromium "Intent to Remove: SwiftShader Fallback," blink-dev, Feb 13 2025 + swiftshader.md docs):** "Allowing automatic fallback to WebGL backed by SwiftShader is deprecated and will be removed. This has been noted in DevTools since Chrome 130. WebGL context creation will fail instead of falling back to SwiftShader." It affects "approximately 2.7% of WebGL contexts," is desktop-only, can be re-enabled via `--enable-unsafe-swiftshader`, and Chromium explicitly advises developers "fall back to other web APIs such as Canvas2D." (WARP is replacing SwiftShader on Windows; Chrome's blocklist blocks llvmpipe, though Firefox allows it.) **Implication: build a genuine non-WebGL 2D fallback** (Canvas 2D / SVG / DOM) for the coverage field — on current Chrome the 3D path may simply fail to initialize on a no-GPU machine. A Canvas-2D coverage grid also happens to be the most defense-safe, highest-legibility rendering of your core "field turns green" moment.

**Recommendation for your renderer architecture:** Treat the 2D representation as primary and the 3D as an enhancement. A DOM/SVG/Canvas-2D coverage field with dots + a simple 3D orbit view (unlit, DPR 1, on-demand, no AA) that degrades gracefully. This satisfies both audiences and survives the venue machine.

### Area 8 — LEO constellation + beam-footprint visualization prior art

- **starlink.sx (Starlink Coverage Tracker).** Deployed community exemplar, the closest to your domain. It "plots the contour of the 3-dB spot beam over the customer's location," shows H3 cells under the beam, follows a satellite's path, shows altitude/azimuth/elevation, and — notably — has **"capacity simulation options… set minimum and maximum gateway capacities… Ku band service beam maximum capacity."** This is prior art for visualizing *beam footprints and capacity limits* together. Its own warning ("resource-intensive, slow devices could suffer; not mobile friendly") underscores your performance constraint.
- **satellitemap.space and issinfo.net.** Deployed. issinfo.net explicitly renders "around 6,000 satellites… as a single batched draw call so the page stays smooth" using SGP4 propagation — direct confirmation that batched/instanced rendering is the community-standard approach for the constellation layer (helps CPU submission even under software rendering, though fill-rate still governs).
- **stuffin.space / Celestrak / Heavens-Above.** Deployed satellite-tracker UIs — reference for orbit visualization and camera framing, less so for beams.
- **Beam-hopping academic viz (MDPI Sensors 2022 "Multi-Dimensional Resource Allocation for Beam-Hopping"; PMC "Adaptive Beam Radius"; arXiv 2205.10721; arXiv 2411.08896 Digital-Twin LEO).** Peer-reviewed. These confirm the domain fundamentals (illumination patterns, greedy vs optimized allocation, fairness-between-cells) and that "greedy heuristics may be myopic and struggle to balance throughput, delay, energy efficiency and fairness" — a citable justification for *why your naive baseline collapses* and why a smart policy wins. They also show antenna radiation patterns and beam-radius/gain trade-offs you can simplify into visuals.
- **Energy-efficiency metaphors.** LEO energy-efficiency literature (ScienceDirect "traffic-aware payload switch-off"; TU Wien beam-hopping thesis) frames on-board power as a hard constraint and battery lifetime as an objective. Convention in dashboards is the **battery icon / power bar / heat metaphor**. For you: a per-satellite power bar that drains faster under the naive policy makes the energy-efficiency objective visceral and ties directly to your multi-objective DQN weight.

**Ranked shortlist (Area 8):** (1) starlink.sx — beam contours + capacity simulation, the nearest deployed analogue; (2) issinfo.net/satellitemap.space — proven batched-rendering approach for the constellation; (3) beam-hopping academic viz — domain-accurate justification for baseline collapse and energy metaphors.

## DO / DON'T (overall)

**DO**
- Open with a felt problem (protagonist loses signal) before any jargon; structure the narrative with BUT/THEREFORE.
- Make the aggregate improvement legible through every dot turning green and physically redistributing; keep Jain's index/coverage-% as secondary readouts.
- Let players fail first ("you be the algorithm"), then reveal the RL policy — this is the productive-failure result.
- Use counterfactual sliders (objective weights, capacity, users) as both teaching tool and live rebuttal engine.
- Build a Canvas-2D/SVG coverage field as the primary, defense-safe rendering; treat 3D as an enhancement.
- Cap DPR to 1, disable AA, render on demand, use unlit materials, push text to DOM overlays; detect the software renderer and degrade.
- Seed all randomness; rehearse on GPU-disabled hardware; keep a recorded backup and a pivot line.

**DON'T**
- Don't lead the lay audience with a scalar fairness number — it's the "hollow number" you're trying to avoid.
- Don't rely on automatic SwiftShader fallback on Chrome (being removed) or assume the 3D context will initialize on a no-GPU machine.
- Don't optimize primarily for draw calls on software WebGL — fill-rate/overdraw dominates.
- Don't add interactivity that goes unused or distracts (Distill's warning); every control should earn its place and be self-evident (PhET).
- Don't render all ~100 user labels every frame; cap to the protagonist + nearest few.
- Don't cite the vendor marketing statistics (Advids conversion figures, botbrowser CPU figure, the 6-fps llvmpipe number) as authoritative — they are directional at best.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Lock the core "aha" (do first).** Build the 2D coverage field (one dot per user, red/green) with the basic↔smart toggle and an animated redistribution of dots. Add Jain's index and coverage-% as *secondary* readouts. This is the emotionally and analytically load-bearing moment for both audiences; get it working in Canvas 2D/SVG first, independent of any 3D. *Benchmark to proceed:* a naïve viewer can articulate "the smart one serves everyone" within 10 seconds, unprompted.

**Stage 2 — Add the productive-failure mini-game.** "You be the algorithm": let players assign users to beams, watch beams saturate, score them, then reveal the RL policy's score. Curate a fixed level order (one new "wrinkle" per level, Zachtronics-style); scaffold implicitly (PhET). *Benchmark:* players who fail first should, in informal testing, explain *why* one beam collapsing hurts the tail — the transfer marker.

**Stage 3 — Build the counterfactual slider layer.** Objective-weight, capacity, and user-count sliders that visibly re-shuffle the field (What-If-Tool pattern). Ensure each slider produces a legible, deterministic flip. Add the protagonist-user handover as a time-controllable, slowed-down event with a DOM label. *Benchmark:* you can rebut three anticipated committee challenges live by moving one slider each.

**Stage 4 — Harden for the venue.** Implement runtime software-renderer detection → auto-switch to the Canvas-2D mode; clamp DPR to 1, disable AA, `frameloop="demand"`, unlit materials in the 3D view. Seed all randomness. Record a full backup screencast and a click-through deck. Rehearse on a GPU-disabled machine 10 minutes before. *Benchmark to present live:* sustained interactivity (slider→visible update <1 s) on the actual defense hardware; if not achievable, present the 2D mode as primary and keep 3D as an optional flourish.

**Thresholds that change the plan:** If the venue machine cannot hit interactive latency even in 2D mode, drop live manipulation and drive the whole defense from the pre-recorded video with seeded scenarios (still deterministic, still reproducible on request). If Chrome on the venue machine refuses WebGL context creation (SwiftShader removal), the 2D fallback becomes the *only* path — which is why it must be built first, not last.

## Caveats

- **Source-quality flags.** Live-demo advice (Area 6) and several three.js performance tips (Area 7) come from practitioner blogs/forums and vendor pages, not peer review; they are consistent across many independent reports, so treated as reliable craft knowledge, but the specific numeric marketing claims (e.g., Advids' "conversion +20-30%," a vendor's "49% CPU reduction," the "6 fps" llvmpipe figure) are single-source or vendor figures — directionally useful, not authoritative. Grokipedia entries (Case, Ciechanowski, Zachtronics) were used only to locate primary sources, which were then verified directly.
- **Inferred vs measured (software WebGL).** The material/antialiasing wins are strong inferences from the documented fragment-cost principle, not isolated SwiftShader benchmarks; validate on your target machine.
- **Domain-viz scarcity.** There is no deployed, layperson-facing LEO *beam-allocation* explorable to copy wholesale — you are combining an under-served domain (Area 2/8) with a mature pattern language (Areas 1/3/4/5). That is the novelty and the risk.
- **The biggest external contradiction** is between the "3D is impressive" instinct and the hard evidence that your target hardware punishes 3D fill-rate. Resolve it decisively in favor of a 2D-primary, 3D-enhancement architecture; every source on software rendering points the same way.
